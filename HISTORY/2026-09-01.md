**📚 Day 2: The Dot‑Com Crash 2000 – Tech Hubris**  

**Why This Specific Aspect Matters:**  
The 2000 crash wasn’t just a market correction; it was the moment the “any‑thing‑with‑a‑dot‑com” narrative collided with the reality that revenue, cash flow, and sustainable business models matter. For today’s investors, the lesson is that hype‑driven valuations can implode when the underlying economics are ignored—an echo that resurfaces whenever a new tech wave (AI, quantum, biotech) inflates expectations beyond cash‑generating capacity.

**The Core Concept (5‑Minute Explainer):**  
Imagine a bustling marketplace where every stall sells “magic beans” that promise to grow into giant trees overnight. The crowd—driven by FOMO and speculative fervor—pushes bean prices far above any rational value, betting that the beans will become towering oaks. In reality, many beans are just seeds with no soil, no water, and no chance of sprouting. When the truth surfaces—that the “magic” is a myth—the crowd panics, the bean prices collapse, and the few who held solid, rooted saplings (companies with real revenue) survive, while the rest are left with worthless husks.  

In 1999‑2000, the “beans” were internet companies with vague business models, sky‑high P/E ratios, and little to no profit. The “market” (NASDAQ) surged from ~1,000 to over 5,000 points, driven by venture capital, retail hype, and the belief that the internet would rewrite the rules of commerce. When the Federal Reserve began tightening monetary policy and investors realized the lack of cash flow, the bubble burst. The NASDAQ fell more than 78% from its March 2000 peak to its 2002 trough, wiping out trillions in market value and teaching a generation that **valuation without fundamentals is a recipe for ruin**.

**The Counterintuitive Truth:**  
Everyone blames “over‑optimism” or “greedy investors,” but the deeper, less‑talked‑about error was **the misallocation of capital to *infrastructure* versus *business model***. Many investors poured money into the *platform* (the internet backbone, data centers, fiber) believing it would be the lasting winner, while the actual *value creators*—the companies that could monetize that infrastructure—were often untested. The crash showed that **the real risk isn’t the technology itself, but betting on the wrong layer of the stack**. The winners (e.g., Amazon, eBay) survived because they built a *sustainable revenue engine*, not because they were pure “tech” plays.
