## ⚛️ The Core Concept (5-Minute Explainer)

### Fission: The Old Guard Gets a Makeover

Nuclear fission — splitting heavy atoms like uranium-235 to release energy — has powered the world since the 1950s. A single uranium fuel pellet (the size of your fingertip) contains as much energy as 1 ton of coal. It runs 24/7 regardless of weather, produces zero carbon emissions during operation, and has the highest **capacity factor** (~93%) of any energy source — meaning it's actually producing power 93% of the time. Compare that to solar (~25%) and wind (~35%).

**So why did nuclear stall?** Three reasons:

1. **Cost overruns.** Traditional large reactors (1,000+ MW) are megaprojects. They routinely run 2-3x over budget and 5-10 years behind schedule. Vogtle Units 3 & 4 in Georgia were supposed to cost $14B and ended up at $35B. That kind of risk terrifies utilities and investors.

2. **Political and public fear.** Chernobyl (1986), Fukushima (2011), Three Mile Island (1979) — these names became cultural shorthand for nuclear danger. Even though nuclear is statistically the *safest* energy source per TWh produced (fewer deaths than coal, oil, gas, even wind and solar), the emotional weight is enormous.

3. **Regulatory burden.** The NRC licensing process for a new reactor design in the US can take a decade. That's not safety — that's timeline risk.

**The comeback is built on two shifts:**

- **Small Modular Reactors (SMRs):** Instead of building one giant $30B reactor, companies like **NuScale (SMR/的私人公司 now), Oklo (OKLO), and Kairos Power** are designing compact reactors (50-300 MW) that can be factory-built and shipped on trucks. Think of it like the shift from mainframe computers to distributed servers. Lower upfront cost, faster deployment, less financing risk.

- **Generation IV Designs:** New reactor types use different coolants (molten salt, liquid sodium, helium) that are inherently safer — they can't melt down the way traditional water-cooled reactors can. Some designs can even *consume* existing nuclear waste as fuel.

### Fusion: The "Always 30 Years Away" Dream Gets Closer

Fusion does the opposite of fission — it *combines* light atoms (hydrogen isotopes) into helium, releasing enormous energy. It's what powers the sun. The fuel is virtually unlimited (deuterium from seawater, lithium for tritium breeding). No long-lived radioactive waste. No meltdown risk.

**The challenge:** You need to contain plasma at 150 million °C — hotter than the sun's core — and keep it stable long enough for more energy to come out than you put in.

**What changed recently:**

- In December 2022, Lawrence Livermore's **NIF** achieved net energy gain for the first time — they got 3.15 MJ out from 2.05 MJ of laser energy in. It was a scientific landmark but far from commercial (the lasers consumed ~300MJ of wall-plug electricity).

- Private fusion companies have raised **over $6 billion** collectively. Key players:
  - **Commonwealth Fusion Systems (private)** — MIT spinoff using high-temperature superconducting magnets. Backed by Bill Gates, Google, and others. Targeting a pilot plant by ~2030.
  - **TAE Technologies (private)** — 25+ years old, backed by Google, Chevreul (Chevron's VC arm), and Sumitomo. Pursuing a boron-hydrogen approach.
  - **Helion Energy (private)** — backed by Sam Altman ($375M personally). They're going straight to electricity generation (heat a plasma, capture expanding magnetic flux). Sam Altman is literally betting Microsoft's AI future on this.
  - **Tokamak Energy (UK, private)** and **First Light Fusion (UK, private)** — different approaches, all racing.

**The realistic timeline:** Most credible players target **demonstration plants in the early 2030s, commercial in the mid-2030s**. That's not tomorrow, but it's not science fiction either — it's within the window of LEAPS options you might consider.
